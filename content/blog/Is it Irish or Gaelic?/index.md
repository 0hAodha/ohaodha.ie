## Introduction
Ireland has two official languages, as stated in the Constitution of Ireland (*Bunreacht na hÉireann*):
the first official language and the national language is **Irish** (*Gaeilge*), and **English** (*Sacs-Bhéarla*) is recognised as the second official language[^1].
This distinction is not just a matter of naming: in the event that the wording of a law differs in Irish or has a different interpretation in Irish than the English version, the Irish version of the law takes precedence over the English[^2][^3].
The names used for the national language in the Constitution are the same that most ordinary Irish people are familiar with: *Irish* in English, and *Gaeilge* in Irish.
So much so, in fact, that if someone refers to the language as *Gaelic* online (as non-Irish people often do), the comments are invariably flooded with corrections making a variety of claims: first and foremost, that the language is called *Irish* in English and *Gaeilge* in itself, and often bolder claims, such as claims that *Gaelic* only ever refers to Scots Gaelic, or that *Gaelic* is a language family and not a language.

While I have no doubt that these commenters are well-intentioned, the fact is unavoidable that they are simply wrong, and commenting on an issue that they don't know a whole lot about with an unwarranted level of confidence.
This is quite a prevalent problem that I have observed in online discourse surrounding the Irish language: ostensibly well meaning, ordinary Irish people, confidently making rather bold (and invariably incorrect) statements about the Irish language, despite not being able to speak it themselves.
I can sympathise strongly with these people as someone who is not a native speaker of Irish myself, especially considering the shockingly poor standard of Irish language education in this country, but the problem of people thinking that they are qualified to confidently make bold statements about the Irish language because they themselves are Irish and have completed some rudimentary Irish language education is endemic, and undeniably the source of a large amount of misinformation online.
There are a large number of incorrect, misinformational claims about the Irish language that have spread from this sort of behaviour (and which I intend to write about in the future), but the key one which I wish to attack today is the claim that *Gaelic* is not a valid term to use to refer to the Irish language.

## Differences between Dialects
![Map of the Irish-Speaking Areas of Ireland (*Gaeltacht*) [Source: <a href='https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User:Angr' title='wikipedia:User:Angr'>User:Angr, English Wikipedia</a>]](./images/Gaeltachtai_le_hainmneacha2.png "Map of the Irish-Speaking Areas of Ireland (*Gaeltacht*) [Source: <a href='https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User:Angr' title='wikipedia:User:Angr'>wikipedia:User:Angr</a>]")

As you can tell from what I've written thus far, *Irish* is the ordinary term used for the language by Irish people within Ireland, and *Gaeilge* is the ordinary term used to refer to the language within itself;
this second statement (regarding the term *Gaeilge*), however, is where we encounter the first large complication, and the source of much misinformation on the Irish language.
The Irish language consists of several, rather different, dialects.
The Irish spoken today and can be broadly classified into three major dialects (which themselves consist of sub-dialects), each associated with a province of Ireland:
**Connacht Irish** (***Gaeilge** Chonnacht*), **Ulster Irish** (***Gaeilic** Uladh*), and **Munster Irish** (***Gaelainn** na Mumhan*).
Each dialect has a different word for the language: *Gaeilge* in Connacht Irish, *Gaeilic* in Ulster Irish, and *Gaelainn* in Munster Irish.
Already, this completely discredits the argument that it's incorrect to refer to the language as "Gaelic", as the native term for the language is quite literally *Gaeilic* (pronounced the same as *Gaelic*) in Ulster.
The fact that the term used to refer to the language varies by dialect is probably not known to the majority of Irish people, as most Irish people cannot speak Irish;
what little Irish the average Irish person *can* speak, as taught to them by the public education system, is the widely-despised standardised form of the language called the Official Standard (*An Caighdeán Oifigiúil*), which aimed to unify the dialects into one cohesive dialect (and, many would say, failed horrifically).
The word for the Irish language in the Official Standard is *Gaeilge*[^4], and hence most Irish people are only aware of the word "Gaeilge" as the native word for the language, and not *Gaeilic* or *Gaelainn*.
Despite the Official Standard being taught to every Irish student in the country, there are no native speakers of the Irish language who use the Official Standard (to my knowledge), instead all speaking a non-standard dialect of Irish.

## Usage of the Term "Gaelic" in English
Despite the fact that I've now shown conclusively that the language is referred to as *Gaeilic* by native speakers, one could argue that this is only within the Irish language itself, and that it's still always incorrect to refer to the language as *Gaelic* in English.
While I think that anyone who seriously argues this is probably just being needlessly contrarian, this point can too be disproved rather easily.
My argument here can be split into two key points: (1) the large numbers of native speakers today who use and favour the term *Gaelic*, even if they don't speak Ulster Irish and (2) the extensive historical use of the word *Gaelic* to refer to the language.

Incidentally, a huge problem with the Official Standard is that many non-native speakers fail to acknowledge that native speakers are the ultimate authority on the Irish language, not a governmental standard:
the authority on a language are those people who maintain the living tradition of that language and speak it on a daily basis.
This problem of arrogance in non-native speakers can be illustrated by a time when I myself fell victim to this sort of thinking:
when I was fourteen years old, I spent three weeks in *Ros Muc* in Connemara, Galway on a *Gaeltacht* course, wherein children learn Irish by immersion.
We stayed in the houses of Gaeltacht natives, and had our meals prepared by the *bean an tí* (landlady).
On one occasion, myself and other students laughed amongst ourselves at the landlady's "mispronunciation" of the word *prátaí* (potatoes) as *fataí* --- the irony, of course, being totally lost on us that we were children who couldn't speak a language presuming to correct a woman who spoke the language natively.
Of course, we were (obviously) in the wrong: the Official Standard spelling of the word as *prátaí* fails to include the Connacht dialect in which the word is always pronounced as *fataí*.
This isn't an isolated example: there are countless examples of this sort of arrogance from non-native speakers.
In the video [Speak Irish Gaelic like a native (Part 1): Getting some basic sounds right. (An bhean & an veain.)](https://youtube.com/watch?v=UGSTw_f1zqc) by [Patchy](https://inv.nadeko.net/channel/UCVkuzjYWfmtr3C-zr3HrABQ) on YouTube (a channel which I highly recommend)[^5], Patchy recounts an incident where a non-native speaker of Irish he knew remarked how the native speakers of Mayo "mispronounce" the placename *Béal an Mhuirthead* to which Patchy retorts **"You've noticed it, but you've somehow thought that the native speakers were wrong and the non-native speakers who taught it to you were right!"**, which is a quotation that cannot be repeated often enough.

## Conclusion
So, tl;dr: "Gaelic" is a perfectly legitimate term to use to refer to the language, but "Irish" is more common, and anyone who claims otherwise likely just doesn't know what they're talking about.
