As a child (and still, upon occasion, to this day), I greatly enjoyed the light entertainment television show *Would I Lie to You?*, which involves two teams of (usually) comedians taking turns to tell improbable tales based upon a prompt from a card, with the prompt either being that of a true occurrence in that comedian's life or a fictitious occurrence that they are just then reading for the first time;
when the story is finished and the opposing team has asked whatever questions they deem relevant, the opposing team then predicts whether they think the story is the truth or a lie, with the real answer then being revealed by the participants who told the tale.

However, there is a subtle grammatical quirk in the English language that allows a viewer to tell whether the story was true or false just before the speaker reaches the end of their revelatory sentence.
This is, in practice, wholly inconsequential, as it only gives away the answer just slightly before the words "truth" or "lie" are uttered, probably only in the order of milliseconds in advance, but I find the grammatical insight to be interesting nonetheless.
I first noticed this quirk as a child but have only recently learned the technical terminology to describe it in words, and I suspect that the majority of viewers of the show will have come to the same conscious or unconscious realisation, even if they too have not yet put it into words.

The quirk occurs because of the words used to categorise the tales on the show: "truth" and "lie".
The word "truth" is (usually) an **uncountable noun**, whereas the word "lie" is a **countable noun**.
- **Countable nouns** are the kind that one most commonly thinks of:
they can be singular or plural, can be described in terms of numbers, and use the **indefinite article** *a*.
The word "lie" is **countable** because one may speak of a single *lie* or several *lies*, and thus the word "lie" is preceded by the indefinite article in its singular form: "*a* lie".
- In contrast, **uncountable nouns** cannot be described in terms of numbers and thus have no plural form, and use the **definite article** *the*.
The word "truth" is **uncountable** because we (usually) don't speak of individual *truths*[^1] but of a single, definitive *truth*: "*the* truth".

[^1]: The plural form "truths" is sometimes used, and in such cases the word is, of course, a countable noun.
In my experience, this pluralisation of the word is rare, and regardless is not relevant to the topic at hand, as the uncountable version of the word is used in *Would I Lie to You?*.
